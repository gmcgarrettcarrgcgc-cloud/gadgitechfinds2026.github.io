Writing a Cover Letter People Actually Want to Read

Most cover letters get skimmed in 6 seconds. This guide shows you how to write one that stops hiring managers mid-scroll and makes them want to meet you.

Writing a Cover Letter People Actually Want to Read

1. Understand the Real Purpose of a Cover Letter

A cover letter isn't a summary of your resume. It's not a formality you check off. It's your voice.

A hiring manager uses your resume to answer: "Does this person have the skills we need?"

They use your cover letter to answer: "Is this someone I actually want to work with? Are they serious about this role? Do they understand what we do?"

That's the real job of a cover letter: to prove you're worth an interview by showing you're thoughtful, prepared, and genuinely interested in this specific company and role. Not just any job - this job.

Keep that in mind as you write. You're not trying to impress them with fancy words or padding. You're trying to show them who you are and why you care.

2. The Three-Part Structure That Works

A great cover letter has three parts: Hook, Middle, Close.

The Hook (2-3 sentences)
Start with something specific and true. Show you've done real research about this company or role. Make them want to keep reading.

Bad: "I am writing to express my strong interest in this position."
Good: "When I read that your team just launched the new mobile app for healthcare providers, I knew this was exactly the kind of problem-solving work I want to do."

The difference: one is generic, one is specific to them. Specificity means you actually care.

The Middle (3-4 paragraphs)
This is where you explain your skills and experience, but frame them around the job's needs, not your resume. Look at the job posting. Find the 2-3 most important things they're asking for. In the middle of your letter, address each one by giving a brief example from your experience.

Example job posting line: "We need someone who can manage multiple projects and keep stakeholders updated."

Your response: "In my last role, I managed three concurrent projects for different clients. I kept all stakeholders updated with weekly emails and monthly in-person check-ins. When a deadline was at risk, I communicated the issue early and worked with the team to find a solution. That taught me that clear communication prevents bigger problems down the road."

That's specific, real, and shows you understand what the job actually requires.

The Close (2-3 sentences)
End with confidence and a clear next step. Don't beg for a response. Show them you're serious and professional.

Bad: "I hope you'll consider me. I'm available whenever you want to talk."
Good: "I'm excited about the possibility of bringing this experience to your team. I'm available for an interview at your convenience, and you can reach me at [phone number] or [email] any time this week."

3. Make It Personal (Without Being Weird)

The biggest mistake people make is trying to sound impressive instead of honest.

Show, don't tell. Instead of saying "I'm a hard worker," show it with a specific example: "I noticed the database wasn't organized, so I spent two weeks categorizing all 5,000 client records and built a search system to find them faster. It cut our lookup time by 75%."

That proves you're a hard worker. And it's interesting.

Use your own voice. Write like you'd speak to a colleague you respect - professional, but genuine. If you wouldn't say it out loud, don't write it.

Research before you write. Spend 20 minutes on the company's website, LinkedIn, and recent news before writing a single word. Find one thing that genuinely interests you about what they do. Mention it in your hook.

4. What Not to Do

Don't summarize your resume. They have your resume. Use the cover letter to add context and show personality, not to repeat what's already there.

Don't use cliches. "I'm a self-starter who works well in team environments" means nothing. Everyone says this. Be specific about what makes you different.

Don't make it about what you want. "I'm looking for a role where I can grow" is about you. "I want to bring my experience in X to help your team do Y" is about them. Focus on what you offer, not what you want.

Don't write more than one page. Hiring managers spend an average of 7 seconds scanning a cover letter before deciding whether to read it. Keep it tight. Three to five short paragraphs maximum.

5. The Editing Process

Write a draft without editing. Get everything out. It will be too long and too formal. That's fine.

Cut it by 30%. Delete every sentence that doesn't directly support your hook, your qualifications, or your close.

Read it aloud. If it sounds stiff or corporate, rewrite those sentences in simpler language.

Check for specifics. Every paragraph should have at least one specific detail - a number, a name, a real example. If a paragraph is all general statements, cut it or add a specific.

Have someone else read it. Ask them: "Does this sound like a real person who cares about this job?" If they hesitate, revise.

The goal is a cover letter that sounds like you - thoughtful, prepared, and genuinely interested. When a hiring manager reads it and thinks "I want to talk to this person," you've done your job.
