Woodworking & Small Builds

Building a Simple Bookshelf With Basic Tools

Learn how to build a sturdy, professional-looking bookshelf in a weekend using just a drill, saw, and level. Perfect for beginners—no carpentry experience needed.

STEP 1: CHOOSE AND BUY YOUR WOOD

Visit a big-box hardware store and look for construction-grade lumber. You want:
— One 2x10 board, 8 feet long (this becomes your two shelves; you'll cut it in half)
— Four 2x4 boards, 8 feet long (these become the vertical supports and back bracing)
— One sheet of 0.75-inch plywood, 2x4 feet (optional, for a backing to hide wall imperfections)

Ask the store to make one cut for you — ask them to rip the 2x10 in half lengthwise (so it becomes two 2x5 boards). Many stores do this free or for a small fee. This saves you a ton of work at home.

Bring the lumber home in a truck or SUV. Let it sit in your room for 24 hours before building — wood adjusts to humidity, and this prevents warping later.

Cost checkpoint: lumber $30-40, pocket hole jig $25-30 (if buying new).

STEP 2: MEASURE AND MARK YOUR CUTS

Lay out all your wood on a flat surface like a garage floor or driveway.

For the two shelves: The 2x10 (now in two pieces) is already the right width. Measure and mark 36 inches on each piece. Mark a line with a pencil. Double-check your measurement — measure twice, cut once.

For the vertical sides: Take two 2x4s. Measure and mark them at 48 inches. These will be your left and right edges. Mark a line.

For horizontal bracing: Cut two more 2x4s at 34 inches each (this is 2 inches less than the shelf width, so they fit between the sides without sticking out).

Use a carpenter's square or speed square to draw straight lines across the board. This guides your saw and prevents crooked cuts.

STEP 3: CUT YOUR WOOD

If you have access to a circular saw: clamp the board to a sawhorse. Line up the blade with your marked line. Push the saw smoothly and let the blade do the work — don't force it. Wear safety glasses.

If you're using a handsaw: same idea. Mark the line clearly. Saw slowly and steadily. It takes longer but works fine.

Pro tip: Cut on the "waste" side of your line (the side you're throwing away). This guarantees your finished piece is the right size.

After cutting, sand the cut edges smooth with 120-grit sandpaper. Rough edges splinter and feel cheap.

STEP 4: ASSEMBLE THE FRAME

Lay the two 48-inch vertical boards flat, parallel to each other, exactly 34 inches apart (measure between them at both ends — they should be the same distance).

Take the two 34-inch horizontal pieces. Align one with the top ends of the verticals, the other 10 inches up from the bottom (this space is for your lower shelf). These horizontals keep the frame square and stable.

Use a pocket hole jig (a simple drill guide you clamp to the board). Drill pocket holes in the ends of each horizontal piece — usually 2-3 holes per joint. Insert pocket hole screws (they're shorter and countersunk, so they hide). Tighten them snug but not gorilla-tight.

If you don't have a pocket hole jig, drill regular pilot holes and use 2.5-inch wood screws instead — they'll stick out slightly but still hold fine.

Check that your frame is square by measuring diagonally corner to corner. Both measurements should be equal (or very close). If they're different, tap the frame gently until it's square.

STEP 5: INSTALL THE SHELVES

Set the first shelf (one of your 2x10 pieces, cut to 36 inches) into the lower horizontal bracing. It should sit flat and level.

Use pocket holes again: drill 2 pocket holes into the top of each horizontal support, through into the bottom of the shelf. Screw them tight. The shelf is now locked down and won't slide.

Install the upper shelf the same way, setting it on the upper horizontal braces.

Check both shelves with a level. They should be perfectly horizontal — if water were poured on them, it wouldn't run off. Adjust by tapping shims (thin wooden wedges) under the shelves before final tightening.

STEP 6: SAND AND FINISH

Sand all exposed wood with 120-grit sandpaper first, then 220-grit for a smooth finish. Sand with the grain, not against it. Wipe off all dust with a damp cloth and let it dry.
