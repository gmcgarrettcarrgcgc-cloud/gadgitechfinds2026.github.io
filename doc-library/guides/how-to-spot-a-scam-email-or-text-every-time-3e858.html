How to Spot a Scam Email or Text Every Time

Scammers are getting smarter, but you don't have to fall for it. Learn the exact red flags that reveal a scam before you click, reply, or give away anything.

How to Spot a Scam Email or Text Every Time

1. Check the Sender's Email Address or Phone Number Carefully

This is your first and most reliable defense.

For emails: Scammers often use addresses that look almost real but aren't quite. Real companies use official domain names. For example, Amazon uses @amazon.com. A scammer might use @amazon-security.com or @amazonsupport-verify.net. The difference is tiny, but it's there.

Look at the full email address, not just the display name. The display name can say anything — "Amazon Support" or "Your Bank" — but the actual address behind it tells the truth. Most email apps let you hover over the sender's name to see the real address.

For text messages: Scammers often use numbers that look like they're from a real company (like your bank) but aren't. Real companies send texts from short codes (usually 5-6 digits) or clearly identifiable business numbers. If you're unsure, hang up and call the official customer service number on your bank card or the company's official website. Never call a number from the text itself.

2. Look for Urgency and Threats

Real companies rarely rush you. Scammers almost always do.

Watch for language like:
- "Act now or your account will be closed"
- "Verify immediately or lose access"
- "Click here within 24 hours"
- "Unusual activity detected — confirm now"
- "Your password will expire today"

Companies know that real problems take time to fix. They won't threaten to lock you out in two hours. If a message makes you feel panicked or rushed, that's a red flag. Take a breath. If it's real, you'll have time to handle it properly.

3. Never Click Links or Download Attachments Without Verification

This is how most scams actually work.

Instead of clicking a link in the message, go directly to the company's website by typing the address yourself in your browser. Or call their official phone number (find it on your bank statement, credit card, or the company's official website). Ask if they sent you that message.

If someone emails you an attachment "for your records" or "to update your information," this is almost always a scam. Real companies don't ask you to download files to verify your account. Delete it.

4. Watch for Requests for Personal Information

Real companies will never ask you to confirm sensitive information via email or text.

Never reply with:
- Your password
- Your Social Security number
- Your credit card number
- Your PIN or security code
- Your bank account details
- Your date of birth or full address (for account verification)

If a message asks for any of these, it's a scam. Period. Real companies already have this information and would never ask you to send it through an unsecured message.

5. Check for Grammar and Spelling Mistakes

This one catches more scams than people realize.

Scammers often work in teams across different countries. They use translation tools or write in non-native English. Real companies have quality control. They don't send messages with typos, odd phrasing, or broken English to their customers.

If a message has multiple spelling errors, strange sentence structure, or awkward wording, it's a red flag. Real Amazon emails don't say "Please to verify your accounts urgently." Real banks don't write "Dear valued customer, confirm your informations."

6. Look for Generic Greetings

Real companies address you by name.

Scams often start with:
- "Dear Valued Customer"
- "Dear User"
- "Hello there"
- "Greetings"

When you have an actual account, companies know your name. They use it. If a message from your bank or Amazon treats you generically, that's suspicious.

7. Hover Over Links Before You Click Them

Most email clients let you see where a link actually goes before you click it.

Hover your mouse over any link (don't click). Look at the bottom left of your screen — it will show the real web address. If the link says it goes to "amazon.com" but the address shown is something like "secure-verify-amazon-account.ru" or a string of random numbers, do not click it.
