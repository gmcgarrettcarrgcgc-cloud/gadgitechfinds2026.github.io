Credit & Debt Repair

How Collections Work and What to Do About Them

A debt collector call can feel like a punch to the gut — but you have more power and more rights than you think. This guide breaks down exactly what is happening and exactly what to do next, step by step.

Getting a call, letter, or email from a "debt collector" is one of the most stressful moments in personal finance — mostly because nobody explains how any of it actually works. Here is the truth: collections is just a business. When you do not pay a bill (credit card, medical bill, phone bill, etc.) for long enough, the original company either hires a collection agency to chase the money for them, or sells your debt outright to a collection company for pennies on the dollar. That collection company then tries to get you to pay the full amount — or more, if fees are added — even though they may have paid very little for your debt.

The first thing to understand is that you have federal legal protections under a law called the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA). This law limits what collectors can do. They cannot call you before 8am or after 9pm. They cannot call you at work if you have told them not to. They cannot threaten you, curse at you, or lie about the amount you owe or the consequences of not paying. They cannot tell your friends, family, or employer about your debt (except to locate you). If a collector breaks these rules, you can report them and even sue.

Step 1: Do not ignore the contact, but also do not panic-pay immediately. Before sending any money, you need to verify the debt is real, correctly assigned to you, and for the correct amount.

Step 2: Request a "debt validation letter" in writing within 30 days of first contact.
