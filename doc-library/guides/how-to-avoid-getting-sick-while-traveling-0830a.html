How to Avoid Getting Sick While Traveling

Travel shouldn't mean catching the next bug going around. Learn the practical steps that actually work to stay healthy on the road, from airport security lines to remote destinations.

How to Avoid Getting Sick While Traveling

The Most Common Travel Illnesses — and Why You Get Them

Travel sickness usually falls into three categories: respiratory infections (colds, flu, COVID-19), traveler's diarrhea (from contaminated food or water), and minor infections from touching contaminated surfaces.

These aren't bad luck. They happen because:
- Your immune system is stressed from travel, poor sleep, and time zone changes
- You're exposed to germs you've never encountered before
- You're in closer contact with more people than usual (airports, planes, hotels)
- You're likely eating different foods and drinking different water than your body is used to
- Your hygiene routine gets disrupted

The good news: most of these are preventable with consistent habits. You don't need to be paranoid or avoid travel entirely — you just need a system.

Before Your Trip: Build Your Immune Foundation

Start preparing your body at least one week before travel.

1. Sleep well and consistently. This is non-negotiable. Aim for 7-9 hours per night in the week before you leave. Poor sleep is the single biggest immune system suppressor, and jet lag will hammer your sleep once you arrive. Getting extra sleep now gives you a buffer.

2. Eat well. Focus on vegetables, fruits, lean proteins, and whole grains. Cut back on sugar and ultra-processed foods, which suppress immune function. Aim for at least three servings of vegetables per day.

3. Stay hydrated. Drink at least 8 glasses of water daily starting now. Most people are chronically dehydrated, and dehydration makes you more susceptible to illness. This habit carries forward during your trip.

4. Exercise lightly. Don't exhaust yourself with intense workouts (that suppresses immunity). Instead, do 20-30 minutes of moderate activity — walking, swimming, yoga — to keep your immune system alert without stressing it.

5. Get a flu vaccine if traveling during flu season. If you're traveling internationally, check what vaccines are recommended for your destinations at least 4-6 weeks before departure. Talk to your doctor or visit the CDC website for guidance specific to your route.

6. Wash your hands thoroughly throughout the day for a full week before travel. This reminds your body how to do it correctly and builds the habit before you're exhausted from traveling.

At the Airport and During Flight: Protect Yourself in High-Risk Spaces

Airports and airplanes are germ factories. Thousands of people pass through them every day, many of them sick.

1. Arrive early enough that you're not rushed. Stress suppresses immunity, and rushing is stressful. Give yourself time to move calmly.

2. Wear a mask during the flight if you want extra protection. This is optional but effective. Bring multiple masks. The air on planes is filtered, but people cough directly on each other. A mask blocks most respiratory droplets.

3. Use hand sanitizer (at least 60% alcohol) after touching common surfaces: armrests, tray tables, bathroom door handles, seats. Better yet, use disinfectant wipes if you can bring them — they're more effective than hand sanitizer alone.

4. Don't touch your face. This is where most respiratory viruses enter your body. If you touch your eyes, nose, or mouth, you're giving the germs a direct path in.

5. Bring your own pillow, blanket, or travel pillow. Airplane textiles are rarely cleaned thoroughly between flights. Using your own reduces exposure to unknown germs.

6. Stay hydrated during the flight. Cabin air is dry and dehydrated mucous membranes are more susceptible to infection. Drink water every hour, not just when you feel thirsty.
